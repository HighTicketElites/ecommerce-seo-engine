Direct answer: A successful pool fire-and-water-bowl project is planned as a small multi-trade system, not as a decorative accessory added at the end. Before the pool deck, pedestals, gas piping, water plumbing, electrical path, or controls are finalized, choose the exact bowl family and configuration, confirm the current manufacturer manual, and coordinate the bowl's mounting footprint, fuel, ignition, water flow, drainage, shutoff access, and service access. The visual design matters, but the utility plan is what determines whether the finished feature works cleanly and can be serviced later.

This guide is for homeowners, pool builders, landscape designers, and contractors planning a new pool or major outdoor renovation. It is intentionally a project-planning guide rather than a substitute for the current model-specific installation manual, local code, or a licensed gas, electrical, plumbing, or pool professional.

Start with one rule: select the exact bowl before rough-in

The biggest planning mistake is deciding on “two fire-and-water bowls” in concept, building the columns, and postponing the exact product choice until the end. Different bowl families can change the mounting footprint, scupper geometry, fuel and ignition requirements, valve access, electrical needs, and the dimensions your trades need to preserve inside the pedestal.

Use the architectural concept to establish the general location and quantity first. Then lock the exact product family and configuration before the trades make permanent decisions. The Outdoor Plus' current fire-water-bowl documentation treats gas, water, mounting, drainage, access, and ignition as coordinated parts of the installation rather than isolated tasks.

If the goal is primarily flame and warmth, a dedicated fire bowl is usually the simpler system. If the visual goal depends on a sheet or stream of water returning to the pool, start in the fire-and-water-bowl collection and plan the hydraulic side from the beginning.

How many fire-and-water bowls should a pool use?

There is no universal “correct” number. The quantity is a design decision first and a utility-capacity decision second. Two matching bowls often work well on symmetrical pool walls or spa transitions. Four can reinforce a larger axis or frame a long pool. A single oversized bowl can make sense when the project has one dominant focal point.

Before increasing the count, evaluate four things together:

  • Sightline: What will the composition look like from the house, main seating area, and the long axis of the pool?
  • Scale: Are the bowls proportionate to the wall, columns, coping, and overall pool width?
  • Total gas demand: The installer must size the fuel supply for the connected load of all features, not just one bowl in isolation.
  • Total water-flow demand: Every spillway adds hydraulic demand. Multiple bowls must be included in the total flow and balancing plan.

A visually balanced four-bowl design can still be a poor technical plan if the utility infrastructure was sized as though only one feature existed.

How to choose the bowl size and visual scale

Do not choose a bowl size from product diameter alone. The useful planning question is: how large should the feature feel relative to the architecture supporting it? Compare the bowl's top width, base or pedestal footprint, height, scupper position, and flame zone against the finished column or wall.

For example, current ResideTerra listings for The Outdoor Plus Maya Powdercoated Steel and Avalon Concrete fire-and-water bowls include 24-, 30-, and 36-inch size options, but the physical base and height change by size. That means the exact selected variant should be confirmed before a mason or pool builder finalizes a pedestal cap or utility opening.

Useful sizing questions before ordering:

  • What is the finished pedestal or wall-cap width after tile, stone, stucco, or coping?
  • Will the bowl visually overhang the support in a deliberate way, or look undersized on it?
  • Where is the scupper relative to the waterline and receiving surface?
  • Can the required gas, water, shutoff, ignition, and service access fit without crowding?
  • Does the selected model's mounting method match the surface being built?

The safest sequence is to design the support around a verified product drawing rather than trying to force a bowl onto a finished support later.

Placement: design for the view and for maintenance access

Fire-and-water bowls are often selected for their nighttime effect, so placement should be checked from more than one viewing position. Stand where the project will actually be experienced: inside the house looking out, at the outdoor kitchen, at the lounge area, at the far end of the pool, and at the main approach.

Then do a second pass from the installer's point of view. The current Outdoor Plus manual calls for a location with good drainage and access for installation and maintenance. That matters because a bowl may need future access to gas connections, valves, ignition components, water fittings, and mounting hardware. A beautiful detail that permanently buries service points can turn a routine repair into masonry demolition.

Clearance from structures, combustibles, and overhead obstructions is not a styling preference. The current manual provides clearance diagrams that vary with heat output. Treat the exact current model manual as the controlling source rather than copying a single clearance value from another product or an older installation.

Water-flow planning: the spillway needs its own hydraulic calculation

The water side of the system deserves the same attention as the fire side. The Outdoor Plus match-lit fire-water-bowl manual shows a dedicated water line to the scupper with a flow-control valve, separate from the gas line. The scupper installation section also shows the water line connecting to the scupper fitting. This is a strong reminder that the water effect is not created simply by placing the bowl over a pool; it needs a planned supply path and a way to regulate the flow.

Do not use one universal GPM-per-inch rule for every scupper. The Outdoor Plus has published different flow guidance for different scupper references. A 2023 general scupper planning flyer uses approximately 1.25 GPM per inch of scupper width, while a newer Smooth Flow Scupper specification lists 7–9 GPM for every 12 inches. Those figures are useful as examples of manufacturer planning guidance, but they are not interchangeable.

Gas planning: natural gas vs. propane

Many fire-and-water-bowl families can be ordered for natural gas or propane, but the correct fuel configuration must be chosen before installation. The current Outdoor Plus manual specifically warns the installer to verify the fuel type and supply pressure and states that fuel-line sizing is the installer's responsibility based on the maximum BTU requirement of the product.

Match-lit vs. electronic ignition changes the rough-in

Ignition selection is not simply an upgrade chosen at checkout. It changes how the project is controlled and what infrastructure may be required.

Match-lit systems are mechanically simpler, but still require proper gas shutoff, fuel verification, access, and a safe operating location. Electronic ignition systems add electrical/control requirements and should be coordinated before concrete or masonry closes the pathway.

The Outdoor Plus publishes separate current installation documentation for match-lit and 12V electronic fire-water-bowl systems. That is exactly why a project should not rough in “for a fire bowl” generically. The selected ignition system determines which manual the trades should be following.

Before construction, decide:

  • Match-lit or electronic ignition?
  • Where is the gas shutoff and flame-control access?
  • If electronic, where does the required power/control equipment live?
  • How will the user turn the feature on and off?
  • Can electrical components remain accessible and installed in the environment required by the current manual?

Material and shape affect both design and long-term expectations

ResideTerra currently carries The Outdoor Plus fire-and-water bowls across materials including GFRC concrete, hammered copper, and powder-coated metal. The right material is not only an aesthetic choice.

  • GFRC concrete: useful for projects that want a substantial architectural look and broad finish selection. Confirm the exact model weight, base, finish, and installation details.
  • Copper: creates a warmer, more organic material expression and naturally changes appearance over time. That evolving patina should be treated as part of the design rather than a defect.
  • Powder-coated metal: can give the project a cleaner contemporary profile and a defined finish palette. Confirm the current finish options and care requirements on the exact model.

Shape also changes the composition. Square Maya-style forms tend to reinforce linear modern architecture, while round or tapered forms such as Cazo-style bowls can soften a pool's geometry. Avalon-style bowls provide another architectural profile. The best choice is the one that fits the pool's design language and the required utility/mounting plan.

A practical preconstruction workflow

  1. Define the architectural effect. Decide whether the project needs flame only or flame plus moving water, then establish approximate quantity and locations.
  2. Select the exact product family. Do not stop at “24-inch square bowl.” Choose the manufacturer, model, size, material, fuel, and ignition configuration.
  3. Pull the current manufacturer documents. Verify that every trade is working from the same current manual and specification information.
  4. Coordinate utilities. Gas contractor confirms load and routing; pool/plumbing professional confirms flow and hydraulic design; electrician/control contractor confirms requirements for the selected ignition system.
  5. Confirm structure, drainage, clearances, and service access. Build the pedestal or wall around the actual product requirements rather than assumptions.
  6. Rough in only after the inputs are shared. One field change—such as switching from match-lit to electronic—should trigger a recheck before permanent work continues.
  7. Revalidate before final installation. Check that the ordered SKU, current manual revision, and field conditions still match the plan.

Who this type of feature is a strong fit for

Fire-and-water bowls make the most sense when the moving-water effect is part of the pool design itself, the project has enough utility planning time, and the homeowner values the visual impact enough to justify the added hydraulic and installation coordination.

They are especially well suited to new pools, major pool renovations, raised pool walls, spa transitions, resort-style backyards, hospitality-style residential spaces, and projects where a landscape designer or pool builder is already coordinating multiple trades.

When a simpler fire bowl may be the better choice

A fire-and-water bowl may be the wrong choice if the project is already complete and there is no practical water-supply path, the pool equipment cannot reasonably support the additional water effect, the homeowner does not want the added maintenance/coordination, or the design does not actually benefit from moving water.

In those cases, a flame-only bowl can deliver much of the architectural fire effect with fewer systems to coordinate. Browse the fire bowl collection if the water component is not central to the project.

Frequently asked questions

How many fire-and-water bowls should I put around a pool?

There is no fixed number. Start with the pool's architecture and main sightlines, then confirm that the gas and hydraulic systems can support the total connected demand. Two bowls often create symmetry; four can frame a larger pool; one larger bowl can serve as a singular focal point.

How much water flow does a fire-and-water bowl need?

It depends on the exact scupper and system design. The Outdoor Plus has published different planning values for different scupper references, so do not apply one GPM-per-inch formula universally. Verify the current flow guidance for the exact bowl/scupper selected, then have the pool professional account for elevation, pipe runs, fittings, head loss, balancing, the number of features, and the rest of the pool-system demand.

Do fire-and-water bowls need a separate water line?

The current Outdoor Plus fire-water-bowl manual illustrates a dedicated water line to the scupper with a flow-control valve, separate from the gas line. Follow the current manual for the exact bowl and have the pool/plumbing professional design the hydraulic path.

Can fire-and-water bowls use propane?

Many current The Outdoor Plus families are offered in natural-gas or propane configurations. The correct fuel option must be ordered, and the installer must verify fuel type, pressure, connected load, and line sizing for the exact product.

Should I choose match-lit or electronic ignition?

Choose based on the control experience and infrastructure you want. Match-lit is mechanically simpler. Electronic ignition adds electrical/control requirements and should be selected before rough-in so the project can accommodate the current manufacturer's installation requirements.

Can the pool builder install the entire fire-and-water-bowl system?

That depends on licensing, local requirements, and project scope. These installations can involve pool hydraulics, gas, electrical/control work, masonry, and product-specific mounting. The current manufacturer documentation calls for qualified installation, and the applicable licensed professionals should handle the work under local code and the authority having jurisdiction.

When should the bowl be ordered?

The product does not necessarily need to be physically on site before every planning decision, but the exact model, size, fuel, ignition, and current specification documents should be locked before permanent rough-in and pedestal dimensions are finalized.
